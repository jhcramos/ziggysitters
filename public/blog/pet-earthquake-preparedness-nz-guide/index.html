Pet Earthquake Preparedness in New Zealand: A Practical Guide

Published 22 August 2026 · 9 min read · ZiggySitters NZ

New Zealand households prepare for earthquakes, but pets are often left out of the plan. A frightened dog may bolt through a gate, a cat may hide in an unfamiliar cupboard, and a sitter may not know where the carrier or medication is. A few practical steps now can protect your pet and give your pet sitter a clear plan.

Why pets need their own earthquake plan

During shaking, animals commonly react before or differently from people. Dogs may bark, pace or try to escape. Cats may disappear under furniture and refuse to come when called. Birds and small animals can be injured by falling objects or damaged enclosures. After an earthquake, broken glass, fallen fences, leaking gas, contaminated water and aftershocks create additional hazards.

Your pet may also be impossible to move immediately. A normally sociable cat can become defensive when frightened, while a dog that is hurt may bite even if it has never shown aggression. The safest plan is specific: where the pet goes, who handles them, what equipment is used and when a vet is called.

Build a New Zealand pet emergency kit

Keep supplies in a waterproof container near an exit, while storing a second small kit in the car if practical. Aim for at least seven days of essentials:

  • Food in sealed, labelled containers and a manual can opener
  • Several litres of water, plus collapsible bowls
  • Leads, harnesses, spare collars and a sturdy carrier
  • Medication, prescriptions and a basic first-aid kit
  • Waste bags, litter, paper towels, blankets and familiar bedding
  • Recent photos, microchip number, vaccination record and vet details
  • A torch, power bank, batteries and a waterproof contact card
Make access obvious: Put a sticker near the front door showing how many pets live at the property and where their carriers are kept. Do not rely on a phone app if power or mobile networks are unavailable.

Reduce hazards inside the home

Walk through each room at your pet’s eye level. Secure tall bookcases, televisions and heavy furniture. Move glass, chemicals, knives and hanging objects away from sleeping areas. Lock cupboards containing cleaning products and keep food storage closed. For cats, check that window screens and cat flaps cannot become escape routes during panic. For aquariums, place them on a stable stand and keep a covered bucket nearby for emergency relocation.

Identify a safer interior location away from windows and large unsecured objects. A pet may hide there naturally if you make it comfortable with a mat, treats and a familiar toy. Never force a frightened animal out during shaking unless there is an immediate danger.

What to do during shaking

Follow the same core safety advice as people: drop, cover and hold. Do not chase a bolting animal, stand in a doorway with a pet, or run outside while objects are falling. If your dog is beside you, keep the lead close without wrapping it around your hand. If your cat is in a carrier, keep the carrier stable and covered with a light blanket. Birds and small pets should remain in secure enclosures unless the enclosure itself is unsafe.

After the earthquake: check, contain and communicate

When shaking stops, check people first, then assess your pet calmly. Look for bleeding, limping, breathing difficulty, burns, unusual weakness or signs of shock. Do not give human pain medication. Call your vet or an after-hours animal emergency service for advice, and transport an injured pet in a carrier or supported crate.

Keep animals on a lead or inside a carrier until fencing, doors and windows are confirmed secure. Familiar landmarks may be damaged, and noise can trigger escape. Use bottled water if the supply is questionable. Expect aftershocks and keep the emergency kit accessible.

Urgent signs: difficulty breathing, collapse, uncontrolled bleeding, pale gums, seizures, suspected poisoning, inability to stand or severe pain require urgent veterinary advice. A sitter should have written authorisation to seek treatment and a spending limit before you travel.

Give your pet sitter a written earthquake plan

Before a holiday, show your sitter the kit, safe area, shut-off locations and escape routes. Write down your vet, nearest after-hours clinic, microchip details, medication schedule and an alternative contact. Explain whether the sitter should evacuate with the pet, where to meet, and how you want updates handled if networks are overloaded. Include a paper copy in the kit.

Ask the sitter to photograph the pet after a major event, confirm food and water, and report injuries or changes in behaviour. If you are unreachable, they should prioritise immediate safety and veterinary care rather than wait for permission. A clear agreement prevents dangerous hesitation.

Practice once before you need it

Do a short drill: locate the carrier, put on the harness, move to the safe area and check the kit. Reward calm behaviour. Practise loading the car if your pet will evacuate that way. Update the food, medication and contact details every few months, especially after moving house or changing vets.

Include identification in more than one form. A collar tag can help a neighbour return a dog quickly, while a current microchip registration remains important if a frightened cat or dog travels further. Check that your contact number and an alternate contact are up to date before leaving. Keep a printed photo with the emergency documents because phones may be flat or damaged.
