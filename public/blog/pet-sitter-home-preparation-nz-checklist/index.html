Preparing Your Home for a Pet Sitter in NZ: Complete Checklist

Published 29 August 2026 · 9 min read

A good pet sitter can keep your dog, cat or small animal comfortable while you are away, but preparation determines how smoothly the booking runs. The goal is not to turn your home into a manual. It is to remove guesswork: where the food is, what the normal routine looks like, which gate is secure, who the sitter calls and what to do if something goes wrong. This New Zealand pet-sitting checklist covers the practical details owners most often forget.

Quick Answer: What should I prepare?

Before your sitter arrives, write a daily care sheet, portion food and medication, test keys or access codes, remove hazards, secure gates and windows, and leave vet and emergency contacts. Arrange a meet-and-greet in the home and, for longer bookings, consider a short trial visit. Good instructions are specific enough that a tired sitter can follow them at 7 am or during a power cut.

Simple test: ask someone unfamiliar with your routine to follow the care sheet. Every question they ask identifies a missing instruction.

One week before: create the care pack

Use one folder, digital note or labelled binder. Put the urgent information on page one and detailed routines after it. Include your address, alarm instructions, Wi-Fi if the sitter is staying overnight, your travel dates and the best way to contact you across New Zealand or overseas.

  • Pet names, ages, microchip numbers and distinguishing marks.
  • Feeding times, exact quantities, treats and foods to avoid.
  • Medication name, dose, method, timing and storage.
  • Usual toilet habits, walk length, commands and triggers.
  • Vet name and phone number, after-hours clinic and a local backup person.
  • Permission and spending limit for emergency veterinary treatment.

Leave the vet’s contact details even if your pet is healthy. Clinics may be closed overnight or on public holidays, and a sitter should not have to search while an animal is unwell. Tell the vet in advance if someone else may bring your pet in.

Food, medication and supplies

Buy enough food for the booking plus a buffer for delays. Measure meals into containers labelled by day or feeding time. This is especially useful for multi-pet homes where one animal needs a prescription diet. Store treats with a written daily limit; “a few” means different things to different people.

Put leads, harnesses, poo bags, towels, brushes and cleaning products in one obvious location. Check that collars and identification tags are readable. For cats, provide extra litter and show where replacement bags, scoop and rubbish bins are kept. For rabbits, birds, fish or reptiles, list temperature, lighting, feeding and enclosure requirements separately.

Medication should remain in original packaging, with a written dose and a record sheet. Demonstrate tablets, ear drops, insulin or topical treatments during the meet-and-greet. If a task is too technical for a general sitter, organise veterinary support rather than hoping it will be fine.

Make the property safe

Walk through the home at the pet’s eye level. Secure cleaning chemicals, human medicines, rat bait, snail bait, fertilisers, chocolate, onions, grapes and xylitol products. Move electrical cords and small objects out of reach. Check balcony rails, pool access, screens and windows. New Zealand homes can also contain hazards in garages, sheds and gardens, including antifreeze, compost, toxic plants and unsecured tools.

Test every gate and latch. Tell the sitter which doors must never be opened and whether visitors, tradespeople or children will be present. If your dog is a gate-dasher, use a second barrier and add a note by the exit. Keep cats indoors if that is their normal routine; never assume a sitter will know local road, wildlife or council restrictions.

Prepare for weather. Leave a warm, dry sleeping area for winter, shade and water for summer, and instructions for heat, storms or flooding. In regions exposed to earthquakes, keep the pet carrier, lead, food, water and medication where they can be reached quickly. Include the household evacuation plan and the name of a pet-friendly backup location.

Access, privacy and house instructions

Decide whether the sitter will visit, stay overnight or collect the pet. Test keys in the actual lock and check the garage remote battery. If using a smart lock or alarm, demonstrate it and leave a physical backup where appropriate. Do not hide access instructions in an obscure message thread.

Be clear about boundaries. Identify rooms that are off-limits, security cameras, parking arrangements, rubbish day, mail, plants and whether the sitter may have visitors. If cameras record inside the home, disclose them before booking and follow applicable privacy expectations. A professional arrangement works best when both owner and sitter know what is expected.

Meet-and-greet: rehearse the normal day

Show the sitter the morning routine from opening the door to returning from the walk. Let them practise harnesses, food preparation, medication and cleaning. Point out your pet’s normal behaviour: how they ask to go outside, where they hide, how much they drink and what noises worry them. Show recent photos if a pet’s weight, coat or appearance has changed.

Discuss realistic updates. Many owners want one message and a photo each day; others need medication confirmation after every dose. Agree what counts as urgent and what can wait until the next update. A calm, agreed communication plan reduces unnecessary anxiety for everyone.

Departure-day checklist

  1. Refresh water bowls and clean litter trays.
  2. Put food, medication and care sheet in their agreed locations.
  3. Remove rubbish, perishables and accessible hazards.
  4. Check gates, doors, windows and alarms.
  5. Leave keys, emergency contacts and backup-person details.
  6. Send your travel itinerary and confirm the sitter’s arrival time.
  7. Record the last meal, toilet break and medication dose.

Do not make a major routine change on departure morning. New food, a long unusual walk or a rushed goodbye can create problems that look like illness or anxiety. Keep the handover calm and give the sitter time to settle the pet.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving only verbal instructions or a food brand with no portion size.
  • Forgetting to disclose a reactive behaviour, escape habit or house alarm.
  • Providing a vet number but no authorisation for emergency treatment.
  • Failing to leave enough food, litter, medication or cleaning supplies for delays.

Key takeaways

  • Put urgent information first and make daily instructions measurable.
  • Rehearse access, medication and safety procedures at the meet-and-greet.
  • Secure indoor hazards, gates, windows and weather-related risks.
  • Agree updates, escalation and emergency spending before you leave.

FAQ

How much food should I leave for a pet sitter?

Leave enough for the booking plus several extra days if practical. Portioning meals and labelling them prevents overfeeding and makes delays easier to manage.

Should a pet sitter have a key?

Yes, if they need home access, but test it first and provide a secure backup plan. Never rely on a key hidden in an obvious outdoor location.

What if my pet needs emergency veterinary care?

Leave the vet’s details, a local backup contact and written authority with a spending limit. Tell the sitter to seek urgent care first when delay could endanger the pet.

Do I need a meet-and-greet?

Yes. It lets the sitter meet the pet in its own environment and exposes unclear instructions before your departure.
