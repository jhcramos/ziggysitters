Rural Pet Sitting in New Zealand: Lifestyle Block and Farm Guide

Published 22 August 2026 · 10 min read · ZiggySitters NZ

Leaving a lifestyle block is different from leaving a suburban home. Your sitter may need to feed chickens, check paddocks, secure gates, monitor water troughs and care for dogs and cats at the same time. The right rural pet sitter can manage a routine; the wrong handover creates avoidable risk. Use this guide to prepare yours.

Decide exactly what “pet care” includes

Start with an inventory, not a vague conversation. List every animal, its location, feeding time, normal behaviour and backup plan. A property may include companion dogs and cats, poultry, rabbits, sheep, goats, alpacas, horses or bees. Some are pets; others may be livestock, and the sitter must know the boundary of their responsibility.

For each animal, record what a normal day looks like. Include feed quantities, supplements, water checks, medications, egg collection, stall or coop cleaning, and whether an animal needs to be separated. A simple map with paddocks, gates, sheds and hazards is often more useful than several pages of prose.

Choose a sitter with the right experience

Not every excellent dog sitter is comfortable entering a paddock with goats or handling poultry. Ask direct questions: Have they cared for farm animals? Can they identify normal versus concerning behaviour? Are they confident around electric fences, tractors, farm dogs and muddy ground? Will they follow biosecurity instructions and keep visitor dogs away from livestock?

For horses, cattle or animals requiring specialised handling, use a qualified or experienced person and clarify the role. A general pet-sitting booking should not silently become veterinary, farriery or farm-management work. If the routine is complex, arrange a second trained contact for emergencies.

Prepare a practical care sheet

Put the following in one waterproof folder and show it during the meet-and-greet:

  • A property map, gate codes and where spare keys are kept
  • Animal names, photos, identification and normal temperament
  • Feed type, measured portions, times and safe treats
  • Medication instructions, storage and missed-dose guidance
  • Vet, farrier, animal health professional and neighbour contacts
  • Instructions for power cuts, water pumps, heat, storms and fire
  • Which gates must remain shut and which paddocks are out of bounds
  • Waste, bedding, egg collection and rubbish procedures
Use measurements: “One scoop” means different things to different people. Weigh or mark the correct amount in a container, label it by animal and leave one day of pre-portioned feed as a visual reference.

Make the property safe for a sitter

Walk the boundaries before departure. Repair loose fencing, latch gates, remove barbed-wire hazards from access routes and identify areas that become slippery after rain. Explain electric fences and isolate them if the sitter needs to work nearby. Store chemicals, poisonous baits, machinery keys and animal medicines securely. Keep dogs separated from unfamiliar visitors and livestock unless the sitter has explicit instructions and the setup is reliable.

Rural properties can have poor mobile coverage. Leave a charged power bank, a landline or radio if available, and a written address with directions for emergency services. Mark the driveway and keep access clear for a vet or fire appliance. Confirm whether the house has tank water, bore water, a pump or generator, and what the sitter should do if one fails.

Seasonal issues across New Zealand

Weather changes the routine. In summer, check water troughs, shade and heat stress, and secure animals before fire-risk conditions worsen. In winter, plan for flooding, slips, snow in alpine areas, muddy access and power cuts. During lambing or kidding, ask an experienced person to take responsibility for monitoring rather than expecting a casual sitter to diagnose complications.

New Zealand properties may also face wasps, toxic plants, contaminated waterways and roaming dogs. Point out known hazards. Do not assume a sitter will recognise a plant, fence fault or livestock illness that is familiar to you.

Emergency instructions that remove guesswork

Write “if this happens, do this” instructions. If an animal is injured, the sitter should secure the area, separate the animal if safe, call the named professional and tell you what has happened. Include permission for emergency veterinary treatment and a spending limit. For escaped animals, give the preferred gate, neighbour and identification details. For a storm or fire warning, explain evacuation priorities and where animals can be moved.

Do not overpromise: A pet sitter can observe, feed, secure and report. They cannot guarantee livestock survival in a flood, repair a failed pump or replace a vet. Agree on a realistic scope and a backup person before you leave.

Set communication expectations

For a rural booking, request a daily check-in covering every animal group, feed and water, gates, weather damage and anything unusual. Photos are valuable: a water trough, a closed gate and an animal’s posture can reveal more than “all good.” Choose one primary channel, tell the sitter when you will be unreachable and provide an alternate contact.

Ask for immediate contact about no appetite, lameness, repeated coughing, injury, escape, broken fencing, loss of water or a dead animal. Early reporting gives your vet or neighbour more options. It also protects the sitter from having to make a major decision alone.

Do a supervised handover

Allow enough time for the sitter to perform the entire routine while you watch. Have them locate feed, open and close gates, check water and demonstrate medication. Correct unclear steps immediately. Leave labelled equipment where it is used, not hidden in a shed the sitter has never entered.

After the handover, send a written summary and ask the sitter to repeat the emergency contacts. This is not bureaucracy; it is a simple test that the plan works when you are on a plane.
