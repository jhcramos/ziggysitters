Understanding Cat Vocalisation: Meows, Purrs, Chirps & What They All Mean

Published 4 August 2026 — ZiggySitters NZ

Cats have one of the most varied vocal repertoires of any domestic animal. They meow, purr, chirp, trill, hiss, growl, yowl, and chatter — sometimes all within a single minute. Yet most cat owners understand only a fraction of what their cat is actually saying. This guide breaks down every common cat vocalisation, what it means, and when you should pay closer attention to what your cat is trying to tell you.

The Meow: A Sound Designed Entirely for Humans

Here is the most surprising fact about cat vocalisation: adult cats almost never meow at each other. Feral cats, wild cats, and free-roaming cats communicate with each other through scent marking, body language, and subtle vocalisations like growls and yowls. The meow is a sound kittens use to call their mothers — and adult domestic cats have learned to direct it at humans because it works.

Research published in Current Biology found that cats use a specific “solicitation purr” that incorporates a high-frequency element similar to a baby cry, which triggers an urgent response in humans. Cats have essentially evolved a sound that is difficult for humans to ignore. When your cat meows at you, they are not just making noise — they are using a communication tool they developed specifically for people.

Types of Meows and What They Mean

The Short, Single Meow

A brief “mew” or single meow is a greeting. Your cat is saying “hello” or acknowledging your presence. You will hear this when you come home, walk into a room, or make eye contact. It is the feline equivalent of a friendly nod. Respond with a gentle greeting back — many cats enjoy a short conversation.

The Mid-Length, Repeated Meow

This is the most common “request” meow. Your cat wants something specific: food, a door opened, attention, or play. The pitch and urgency tend to increase the longer you take to respond. If you notice your cat meows at a specific time each day (like 6am near the food bowl), it is a learned routine — they meow because you have reinforced it by responding.

The Long, Drawn-Out Meow

An elongated “mrrrow” is more demanding. Your cat is not just asking — they are insisting. This often accompanies a specific action: standing by an empty bowl, pawing at a closed door, or circling your legs while you prepare food. It can also express complaint: “Why is this door closed?” or “Why have you not fed me yet?”

The Silent Meow

When your cat opens their mouth as if to meow but makes no sound (or only a tiny squeak), they are not being silent — they are producing a sound at a frequency humans can barely hear. This is usually an affectionate or appeasing gesture. Your cat is saying “please” in the softest way possible. It is almost impossible to ignore, which is precisely the point.

The Chatter Meow (With Jaw Trembling)

You will hear this when your cat is watching birds or insects through a window. The rapid, clicking “ek-ek-ek” sound with jaw movement is called chattering or chattering. Behavioural scientists believe it expresses a mix of excitement and frustration — the cat wants to hunt but cannot reach the prey. Some researchers theorise it mimics the killing bite, practised in anticipation.

The Purr: Not Always a Sign of Happiness

Purring is the sound most people associate with contentment, and it often is. Cats purr when they are relaxed, being petted, or kneading a favourite blanket. But purring has a darker side that many cat owners do not know about.

The Contentment Purr

A slow, steady purr with a relaxed body (half-closed eyes, slow blinking, loose posture) means your cat is comfortable and happy. This is the purr most cat owners know and love. It usually occurs during petting, settling into a warm spot, or nursing kittens.

The Solicitation Purr

This is the “feed me” purr — and it is acoustically different from the contentment purr. It incorporates a high-pitched cry component at around 300-600 Hz that is similar to a human baby cry. A study at the University of Sussex found that humans rate solicitation purrs as more urgent and harder to ignore than regular purrs, even when they cannot consciously tell the difference. Your cat is not just purring — they are manipulating you, and it works brilliantly.

The Stress or Pain Purr

Cats also purr when they are in pain, frightened, or unwell. Mother cats purr during labour. Injured cats purr at the vet. This purr serves a self-soothing function and may also have a healing purpose: the frequency of a cat purr (25-150 Hz) has been shown in laboratory settings to promote bone density and tissue regeneration. The hypothesis is that purring is a built-in healing mechanism — a low-energy way to stimulate recovery. If your cat is purring but their body language signals stress (tense muscles, dilated pupils, hiding, hunched posture), the purr is not contentment. It is a coping mechanism.

When to worry: If your cat suddenly starts purring constantly but seems withdrawn, is hiding, has stopped eating, or shows any change in behaviour, consult your vet. Purring combined with signs of illness is a red flag, not a sign that everything is fine.

Chirps and Trills: The Friendly Announcements

Chirps (short, high-pitched “brrrp” sounds) and trills (longer, rolling sounds that rise at the end) are among the most affectionate sounds in a cat vocabulary. Mother cats use trills to call their kittens. When your adult cat trills at you, they are treating you as family — specifically, as a kitten they are greeting or herding.

Common contexts for chirps and trills:

  • Greeting you when you come home — the cat version of “oh, you are back!”
  • Following you from room to room — herding behaviour, keeping their “kitten” (you) safe
  • When they are about to jump or run — an announcement of movement, common in Bengals, Siamese, and other vocal breeds
  • Seeing another cat through the window — a friendly (or sometimes territorial) acknowledgment

Chirps and trills are almost always positive. If your cat makes these sounds, they are happy to see you or engaged with something they find interesting.

Hissing and Growling: The Back-Off Warnings

These are defensive sounds, not aggressive ones. A hissing or growling cat is saying “I am scared and I want you to go away” — not “I want to fight you.” Understanding this distinction is critical.

Hissing

Hissing is a snake-mimicry sound. Cats evolved to produce a sound that resembles a snake hiss because snakes are universally feared by mammals. When a cat hisses, they are saying “I am dangerous, stay back” in a language that even non-cat species understand. Hissing is almost always a defensive response. The cat feels threatened, cornered, or overwhelmed. The correct response is to back away slowly and give the cat space. Do not try to comfort or approach a hissing cat — you will only escalate their fear.

Growling

Growling is a step up from hissing in intensity. It is a low, rumbling sound that signals “I am prepared to defend myself.” Cats growl when they are guarding food, territory, or kittens, or when they are in pain. If your cat growls when you touch a specific part of their body, that is a veterinary concern — it may indicate pain or injury.

Spitting

Spitting is an explosive, high-intensity defensive sound. It usually accompanies a lunge or swipe. If a cat is spitting, they are at their absolute limit of fear and are about to attack if the threat does not retreat. This is rare in well-adjusted pet cats and usually occurs in situations of extreme stress: at the vet, during introductions to new animals, or when a cat is cornered.

Yowling: The Sound That Demands Attention

Yowling is an extended, loud, drawn-out vocalisation that sounds distinctly different from a meow. It is deeper, longer, and more intense. Yowling is always worth paying attention to because it signals something significant.

Why Cats Yowl

  • Mating behaviour: Unspayed females yowl to attract mates during oestrus. Unneutered males yowl in response. If your cat is not desexed, this is the most common cause of night-time yowling.
  • Cognitive dysfunction: Senior cats with dementia (feline cognitive dysfunction syndrome) often yowl at night, seemingly at nothing. They are disoriented and calling out for reassurance. If your older cat has started night-time yowling, schedule a vet check.
  • Pain or illness: Cats in significant pain may yowl. Hyperthyroidism, kidney disease, dental pain, and urinary blockages are common medical causes. A male cat yowling near the litter tray may have a blocked urethra — this is a medical emergency.
  • Territorial disputes: Outdoor cats or cats that can see neighbourhood cats through windows may yowl to assert territory or express frustration.
  • Separation anxiety: Some cats, especially those strongly bonded to one person, yowl when that person leaves or is absent. This is more common than most people realise and often mistaken for “just being vocal.”
Sudden yowling in a senior cat is one of the most common early signs of hyperthyroidism or cognitive decline. Do not dismiss it as “just getting old.” Book a vet appointment. Both conditions are treatable.

The Chatter: Hunting Frustration at the Window

We touched on chattering earlier, but it deserves a deeper look because it is one of the most distinctive and poorly understood cat sounds. When your cat sits at the window making rapid “ek-ek-ek” sounds with their jaw vibrating, they are watching prey they cannot reach.

The chatter is a combination of excitement and frustration. The jaw movement mimics the killing bite — the rapid bite to the neck that cats use to dispatch prey. Some behaviourists believe chattering is literally practise for the kill, while others see it as an expression of thwarted hunting drive.

Indoor cats chatter more frequently than outdoor cats because they see prey (birds, insects, lizards) through windows but cannot access it. If your indoor cat chatters frequently, they may benefit from more interactive play that satisfies their hunting instinct. Wand toys, laser pointers (followed by a real treat), and puzzle feeders can help redirect that frustration.

Breed Differences: Some Cats Talk More Than Others

Cat vocalisation varies enormously by breed. If you have a Siamese, Oriental, Burmese, or Bengal, you already know they will talk your ear off. These breeds have been selected (intentionally or not) for vocal tendencies, and they use a wider range of sounds more frequently than quieter breeds.

Vocalisation by Breed Tendency:
  • Very vocal: Siamese, Oriental Shorthair, Bengal, Burmese, Savannah, Sphynx
  • Moderately vocal: Ragdoll, Maine Coon, Abyssinian, Devon Rex, Scottish Fold
  • Quiet: Persian, British Shorthair, Russian Blue, Norwegian Forest Cat, Chartreux

If you have a naturally quiet breed that suddenly becomes vocal, treat it as a potential health concern. A Persian that starts meowing constantly is telling you something is wrong.

When Vocalisation Changes: Red Flags

Any significant change in your cat vocalisation pattern is worth investigating. Here are the key red flags:

Sudden Increase in Meowing or Yowling

This can indicate hyperthyroidism (especially in cats over 10), pain, anxiety, or cognitive decline. It is the most common reason owners of senior cats bring them to the vet. Do not dismiss it as “just being vocal” — get it checked.

Sudden Decrease in Vocalisation

A normally chatty cat that goes quiet may be in pain, depressed, or ill. Cats in pain often withdraw and become silent. If your vocal cat stops talking, that silence is louder than any meow.

Changes in Voice Quality

If your cat meow sounds different — hoarser, quieter, or strained — it could indicate laryngeal issues, upper respiratory infections, oral tumours, or throat trauma. A voice change that persists for more than a day or two warrants a vet visit.

Night-Time Yowling in Older Cats

This is the classic presentation of feline cognitive dysfunction syndrome (kitty dementia) and hyperthyroidism. Both are common in cats over 10 and both are treatable. A blood panel and physical exam can rule these out or catch them early. Do not accept “old cats just do that” as an answer without investigating.

How to Respond to Your Cat Vocalisations

Understanding what your cat is saying is only half the equation. How you respond shapes their future communication. Here are the principles:

  • Respond to genuine needs, not demands: If your cat meows for food at mealtimes, respond. If they meow for attention at 3am, ignore it (and do not reward it with food or play, or you will train them to keep doing it).
  • Do not punish vocalisation: Yelling at or spraying a meowing cat does not teach them to be quiet — it teaches them you are scary and unreliable. It also increases anxiety, which often increases vocalisation.
  • Talk back: Cats who receive vocal responses from their owners tend to develop richer “conversations.” A short, gentle response to a greeting meow reinforces the bond. Ignoring every meow teaches your cat that vocalisation does not work — which can lead to frustration or silence.
  • Learn their patterns: Every cat develops an individual vocal “vocabulary” with their owner. Over time, you will learn that a specific meow at the front door means “let me out,” while a different meow near the kitchen means “feed me.” Pay attention and respond consistently.
  • Rule out medical causes first: Any sudden change in vocalisation should start with a vet visit, not behaviour modification. Pain, illness, and cognitive decline all change how and how much a cat vocalises.

Leaving a Vocal Cat with a Sitter

If your cat is vocal — especially if they are a Siamese, Bengal, or Oriental breed — they may become more chatty, more demanding, or more anxious when you are away. This is normal. The disruption of routine and the absence of their primary human can trigger increased vocalisation, especially in cats with separation anxiety.

Tips for sitters caring for vocal cats:

  • Learn the cat normal vocal patterns from the owner before they leave
  • Respond to greetings but do not reinforce demand meowing (especially overnight)
  • Be alert for yowling or sudden silence — these may indicate distress or illness
  • Leave a radio or TV on for background noise — this can reduce anxiety-related vocalisation
  • Follow the owner feeding and play schedule exactly to minimise disruption

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my cat meow at me but not at other cats?

Adult cats meow almost exclusively at humans, not at other cats. The meow is a kitten vocalisation that domestic cats have retained (and adapted) specifically for communicating with people. Cats use scent, body language, and other sounds (hisses, growls, yowls) to communicate with each other. When your cat meows at you, they are using a language they developed for humans alone.

Why does my cat purr when they seem stressed or in pain?

Purring is not exclusively a sign of contentment. Cats purr when they are frightened, injured, or even dying. The frequency of a purr (25–150 Hz) has been linked to tissue healing and bone density maintenance. When a cat purrs during stress or pain, it is likely a self-soothing mechanism and possibly an attempt to promote healing. Always check for other signs of distress rather than assuming a purring cat is happy.

My cat has started yowling at night. Should I be worried?

Yes — night-time yowling in a cat that has not done this before is a red flag, especially in cats over 10. The two most common causes are hyperthyroidism and feline cognitive dysfunction syndrome. Both are treatable. Schedule a vet visit, and in the meantime, leave a night light on and consider a Feliway diffuser to reduce anxiety.

Is it normal for cats to chirp and trill?

Absolutely. Chirps and trills are friendly, affectionate sounds that cats use as greetings and announcements. Mother cats trill to call their kittens, and adult cats trill at humans they consider family. Some breeds (especially Bengals, Siamese, and Maine Coons) chirp and trill more than others. It is a sign of a happy, engaged cat.

How can I stop my cat from meowing excessively?

First, rule out medical causes with a vet visit. Then identify the trigger: hunger, attention-seeking, mating behaviour (if not desexed), or anxiety. Do not reward demand meowing with food or attention. Instead, reward quiet behaviour. Establish consistent routines for feeding and play. If the meowing is anxiety-related, address the underlying cause with environmental enrichment, a consistent sitter, or veterinary behaviour support.
