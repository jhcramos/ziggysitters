International Travel Pet Sitting NZ: A Complete Planning Guide

Published 25 August 2026 · ZiggySitters NZ

Going overseas is exciting until you remember that your dog still needs breakfast at 7am, your cat dislikes unexpected changes, and someone must know what to do if a pet becomes unwell. For New Zealand pet owners, arranging care before an international trip is more than finding a friendly face. It is a practical handover that protects your pet, your home and your peace of mind.

This guide explains how to organise a reliable pet sitter in NZ, whether you live in Auckland, Wellington, Christchurch, Hamilton, Tauranga or a smaller regional community.

Choose the right type of pet care

Start with your pet’s normal routine, not the cheapest option. In-home pet sitting is often a good fit for animals that are anxious, elderly, on medication or strongly attached to their territory. The sitter visits at agreed times, or stays in the home, so feeding, toileting, walks and company happen in familiar surroundings.

House sitting with pets can work well for dogs that need overnight company. Drop-in visits may suit independent cats, but confirm how many visits they will receive each day and what happens if a visit is missed. Boarding can be appropriate for social, adaptable pets, although it changes both environment and routine.

Book early for peak travel dates

International travel often overlaps with New Zealand summer, Christmas, Easter and school holidays. Good sitters book out early, particularly in Auckland and Wellington. Contact potential sitters as soon as flights are confirmed. Give the exact dates, including your departure and arrival days, because delays can turn a ten-day booking into an eleven-day booking.

Ask about cancellation terms, additional nights, key handling and what happens if your flight home is delayed. A clear plan prevents awkward decisions from another country.

Prepare a complete pet care sheet

Do not rely on a verbal briefing at the front door. Leave a printed care sheet and send a digital copy. Include:

  • Each pet’s name, age, breed and personality
  • Meal portions, feeding times, treats and foods to avoid
  • Walk routes, lead rules, recall limits and dog park preferences
  • Medication instructions, storage and what to do if a dose is missed
  • Toilet habits, litter tray routine and signs that are normal for that pet
  • Microchip details, council registration information and identification tags
  • Your vet’s name, phone number and clinic address
  • Your travel itinerary and an emergency contact in New Zealand
Useful test: If a tired sitter could not follow the instructions at 6am, rewrite them. Use exact quantities and times rather than “a little” or “morning”.

Arrange a meet and greet

A meet and greet is not a formality. Watch how your pet responds when the sitter enters, handles food and attaches a lead. Tell the sitter about triggers such as visitors, cyclists, livestock, fireworks or separation anxiety. Show where supplies are kept and let them practise the routine while you are still home.

For dogs, take a short walk together. For cats, show hiding places and explain that patience is better than forced contact. Share the house rules clearly: which rooms are off limits, whether pets sleep on furniture and whether the sitter may have visitors.

Make the home easy to manage

Stock enough food, litter, medication and cleaning supplies for the whole trip plus several extra days. Label containers and check that automatic feeders, cameras, cat flaps and heating systems work. Leave spare keys with a trusted person, not only inside a lockbox. Explain the alarm, rubbish collection, mail and any water or power shut-off points.

Remove hazards before leaving: secure poisonous plants and cleaning products, close windows that do not latch, and check fences and gates. For rural properties, explain paddocks, electric fencing, stock and boundaries. A sitter should never need to guess what is safe.

Agree on communication

Decide how often you want updates. A daily message with a photo may reassure one owner while another prefers updates only when something changes. Ask for a message after the first meal, walk or overnight stay. Confirm the best contact method when you are overseas and provide a backup person who can approve treatment or extra costs.

Also agree on an emergency threshold. A sitter should contact the vet immediately for breathing difficulty, collapse, suspected poisoning, uncontrolled bleeding or repeated vomiting. Do not make them wait for a reply if urgent care is needed.

Leave an emergency plan

Write down who can make decisions if you are unreachable. Include permission for the sitter to take your pet to your nominated vet and instructions about payment. Keep transport options available: a secure carrier for cats and small animals, and a towel or harness for dogs. Leave recent photos, especially if a pet could escape.

New Zealand weather can change quickly. Add instructions for storms, flooding, earthquakes and power cuts. Tell the sitter where pets should be secured and how to account for every animal after an evacuation.

When you return

Plan for a calm reunion. Pets may be overexcited, tired or unsettled after a change in routine. Collect keys, check supplies and ask what the sitter noticed. Pay promptly and leave an honest review if the service was good. A reliable relationship is valuable when your next trip appears on the calendar.

Make international travel easier for your sitter

Leave local information that a visitor may not know: the suburb, nearest supermarket, rubbish day, parking rules and the name of a trusted neighbour. If you are crossing time zones, write the actual New Zealand dates and times rather than relying on your phone calendar. A small amount of preparation saves your sitter from chasing answers while you are boarding a flight.

Finally, make sure your contact details work overseas and tell the sitter when you may be offline. The best pet sitting arrangement is predictable: your pets keep their familiar routine, and the person caring for them has the information and authority to act confidently.
