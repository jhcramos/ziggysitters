Senior Pet Sitting in New Zealand: A Practical Care Guide

Published 29 August 2026 · 10 min read

Older pets often cope best when life stays familiar. A senior dog may need a slower walk, a carefully timed tablet and a warm bed rather than a busy boarding environment. An elderly cat may appear independent but still depend on a precise feeding routine, clean litter tray and someone who notices subtle changes. If you are travelling, working away or facing a family event, the right senior pet sitter in New Zealand can protect that routine while giving you honest updates.

Quick Answer: What does senior pet sitting involve?

Senior pet sitting is tailored care for older dogs, cats and other companion animals with reduced mobility, ongoing medication, hearing or vision changes, or age-related anxiety. Choose an experienced sitter who can follow written instructions, give medication safely, monitor appetite and toileting, and respond quickly if your pet’s condition changes. For most older pets, in-home pet sitting is less disruptive than relocation.

Planning rule: book a meet-and-greet and a trial visit before a long trip. Your sitter should practise every unusual task while you are still nearby.

When is a pet considered senior?

There is no single birthday that applies to every breed. Small dogs may remain active into their early teens, while giant breeds can show age-related changes much earlier. Cats are often described as mature from around seven years and senior from around 11, but your veterinarian’s assessment matters more than a label. Watch for slower rising, stiffness after rest, changes in thirst, weight, appetite, sleep, grooming or behaviour.

New Zealand’s climate makes observation important. Cold, damp South Island mornings can worsen stiffness, while hot North Island summers increase dehydration risk. A good sitter adjusts exercise and indoor comfort to the individual pet, not to a generic “senior” checklist.

What to look for in a senior pet sitter

Search profiles for hands-on experience with elderly animals, medication and mobility support. Ask specific questions: Can they give tablets or liquid medicine? Will they notice if a dog refuses breakfast? How do they help a cat into a carrier? Can they keep walks short and avoid stairs? A confident answer should include a process, not just “I love animals”.

  • Reliability: agreed visit times and a backup contact.
  • Observation: appetite, water, urine, faeces, pain and energy recorded daily.
  • Safe handling: non-slip floors, ramps and harnesses used correctly.
  • Communication: concise updates with photos and immediate escalation for concerning symptoms.
  • Local readiness: knowledge of your regular vet and nearby after-hours clinic.

Build a clear care plan before you leave

Write instructions in the order a sitter will use them. Start with a one-page summary on the fridge: feeding times and quantities, medication doses, emergency contacts, the vet’s details, and what “normal” looks like. Add the longer notes separately so important information is not buried.

For medication, keep everything in original packaging, include the prescription label and demonstrate the technique. Record the last dose before departure. Never ask a sitter to improvise a dose or alter medication without veterinary advice. If a medicine must be refrigerated, mark the storage location and provide a measuring syringe where relevant.

For mobility, identify steps, slippery tiles, swimming pools and places where an older pet could become trapped. Keep beds away from draughts but close enough to water. Cats may need a low-sided litter tray; dogs may need more frequent, shorter toilet breaks. Tell the sitter whether lifting is safe and exactly how to support the animal.

Exercise, enrichment and rest

Senior care is not no exercise. It is the right amount of low-impact movement followed by recovery. A 10-minute sniff walk around a quiet reserve may be better than a long route. In Auckland, avoid busy off-leash areas if your dog is unsteady or easily overwhelmed. In Wellington, wind and steep tracks can make a familiar walk unsafe. In Christchurch or Dunedin, cold pavement and frosty mornings may call for a later start.

Indoor enrichment works for cats and dogs: scatter a small portion of food, use a simple puzzle feeder, practise gentle grooming, or offer calm interaction. Stop if the pet shows fatigue, panting, hiding, licking lips or reluctance to continue. The sitter’s job is to preserve wellbeing, not chase an activity target.

Warning signs and emergency instructions

Your sitter should know which changes require a message and which require immediate veterinary help. Contact a vet promptly for repeated vomiting or diarrhoea, refusal of food, marked lethargy, difficulty breathing, collapse, seizures, sudden confusion, inability to urinate, a swollen abdomen or obvious pain. Do not rely on a photo or online guess when an older pet deteriorates quickly.

Give written permission for emergency treatment and agree on a spending limit, but make clear that urgent veterinary care should not wait for a reply if you cannot be reached. Leave transport instructions, a secure carrier, a lead and muzzle if your pet is trained to use one. Keep your phone available and nominate a local backup person.

Common mistakes

  • Booking a sitter who has only cared for healthy young pets.
  • Leaving medication instructions verbally and assuming they were understood.
  • Changing food, beds or walking routes immediately before departure.
  • Scheduling a single daily visit for a pet that needs toileting, medication or monitoring.

Key takeaways

  • Familiar surroundings and predictable routines usually suit older pets.
  • Choose demonstrated skills in medication, observation and safe handling.
  • Use a written care sheet, trial visit and clear emergency authority.
  • Ask for daily updates that cover behaviour as well as photographs.

FAQ

Is in-home sitting better than boarding for an older pet?

Often, yes. Staying home avoids travel and unfamiliar flooring, smells and animals. Boarding can still work for a social, robust senior pet when the facility can provide individual care.

Can a pet sitter give prescription medication?

Many experienced sitters can administer routine medication, but confirm the exact medicine, technique and limits before booking. Ask your veterinarian about any task that requires clinical judgement.

How often should a senior dog be visited?

It depends on toileting, medication, separation tolerance and health. Some need several visits or overnight care; a sitter should agree a schedule from the care plan.

Should I do a trial booking?

Yes. A short trial reveals whether the sitter can follow the routine and whether your pet is comfortable with them.
