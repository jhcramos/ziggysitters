Pet Medication and Pet Sitting NZ: A Safe Handover Guide

Published 25 August 2026 · ZiggySitters NZ

Finding a pet sitter is straightforward until your dog needs tablets with food, your cat needs insulin at a precise time, or an anxious rabbit refuses medication from a stranger. Medication does not have to stop you travelling, but it does require a careful handover. The sitter needs clear instructions, the right supplies and permission to involve a vet if something goes wrong.

This guide is for New Zealand pet owners arranging in-home pet sitting or house sitting. It supports a conversation with your veterinarian; it does not replace veterinary advice.

Start with the prescribing vet

Before booking care, ask your vet whether the medication can safely be administered by a sitter and whether the schedule can remain unchanged during your absence. Confirm the purpose of each medicine, the dose, the route and the length of treatment. Ask what side effects are expected and which signs require urgent attention.

Request a written prescription or treatment summary if appropriate. Keep the clinic’s phone number, after-hours instructions and address with the care sheet. If your usual clinic is in Auckland, Christchurch, Wellington or a rural town, identify the nearest emergency option to the pet’s home as well.

Choose a sitter who is comfortable with the routine

Be direct about the task before accepting a booking. Giving a pill hidden in food is different from applying ear drops, managing an inhaler or injecting insulin. Ask the sitter what experience they have and whether they are willing to practise while you are present.

Never choose a sitter only because they say “it should be fine”. Good care includes asking questions. A sitter should know when to decline a task and call you or the vet rather than improvise.

Create medication instructions that cannot be misread

Write one line per medication. Include the medicine name, strength, exact amount, time, method and whether it must be given with food. Add a photo of the label if useful. For example: “Miso — gabapentin 100 mg — give one capsule by mouth at 8am and 8pm with a small portion of wet food.” Avoid “one tablet” when several strengths are in the house.

Also include:

  • What to do if the pet spits out or vomits a dose
  • Whether the sitter should record the dose immediately
  • Any foods, supplements or medicines to avoid
  • How to handle a missed or late dose
  • Signs of an allergic reaction or adverse effect
  • The date treatment ends and what to do with remaining medicine
Important: The sitter should never double a dose or change the schedule unless your veterinarian instructs them to do so.

Use a simple tracking system

A medication chart prevents the most common error: two people assuming the other one gave the dose. Record the date, scheduled time, actual time, dose and initials. For twice-daily medicine, use a morning and evening column. Leave a pen beside the chart.

For a long booking, ask the sitter to send a photo of the completed chart each day. This gives you visibility without requiring constant messages. A pill organiser can help with some tablets, but keep original packaging available so the medicine can be identified.

Store medicines safely

Follow the label and veterinary instructions for temperature, light and refrigeration. Keep medicines out of reach of children and pets, including dogs that can open cupboards. Do not transfer liquids into unlabelled containers. Measure liquid medicine with the supplied syringe, not a kitchen teaspoon.

Keep a small medication station together with gloves, syringes, treats, towels and waste bags. Separate medicines for different animals and use each pet’s name on the container. If medication requires refrigeration, show the sitter where it belongs and what to do during a power outage.

Practise before you leave

Schedule a rehearsal at the actual times the sitter will use. Let the sitter administer the medication while you watch, then reverse roles so you can see how they plan to do it. Practise restraint gently and stop if the pet becomes distressed. A frightened pet may need a slower technique, a different treat or veterinary advice.

For cats, demonstrate how to towel-wrap safely if your vet recommends it. For dogs, show where to stand and how to avoid turning medicine time into a chase. Never force a mouth open in a way that risks injury.

Plan for missed doses and emergencies

Put the vet’s instructions in writing for common scenarios. If a tablet is dropped, the sitter needs to know whether to use a spare. If a pet refuses food, they need to know whether the medicine may be given separately. If a dose is missed, they should call the clinic or follow the written direction rather than guess.

Call a vet urgently for collapse, breathing difficulty, seizures, severe weakness, suspected poisoning, facial swelling or repeated vomiting. Provide permission for the sitter to seek treatment and leave a payment method or emergency contact. Tell the sitter where the carrier, lead and pet first-aid supplies are kept.

After the booking

Ask the sitter to hand back unused medicine and the completed chart. Compare the remaining quantity with the record. Tell your vet about any side effects, missed doses or changes in appetite and behaviour. Thank the sitter for specific, careful work and keep the instructions updated for the next booking.

Keep the handover calm and practical

Medication is only one part of the care routine. Tell the sitter what the pet normally does before and after treatment, such as drinking water, resting, hiding or asking to go outside. This helps them distinguish familiar behaviour from a genuine change. Keep the environment quiet and use the same bowl, mat and reward routine whenever possible.

Good notes do not overwhelm a sitter; they remove guesswork. A short written plan, a rehearsal and a reliable vet contact give everyone a safer starting point, especially when the owner is several time zones away.
