Therapy Dogs & Emotional Support Animals in New Zealand: The Complete Guide for 2026

Published 2 August 2026 — ZiggySitters NZ

Therapy dogs, emotional support animals (ESAs), and assistance dogs are three distinct categories in New Zealand law and practice — and confusing them can lead to legal problems, access issues, and wasted money on fake certifications. This guide explains exactly what each category means in NZ, what rights you have, how to get certified or recognised, and how to start training your dog for therapy work.

The Three Categories: Assistance, Therapy, and Emotional Support

These terms are often used interchangeably, but they mean very different things under NZ law and in practice. Understanding the distinction is the first step.

Paying for an ESA letter or vest does not grant your dog public access rights. If you need a dog to assist with a disability, the correct pathway is an assistance dog, not an ESA.

Assistance Dogs in New Zealand

Assistance dogs (also called service dogs) are the only category with comprehensive legal protection in NZ. They are trained to perform specific tasks that mitigate their handler\'s disability. For a detailed guide on assistance dogs specifically, see our assistance dogs NZ guide.

Who Qualifies for an Assistance Dog?

Under the Human Rights Act 1993, a person with a disability is entitled to be accompanied by an assistance dog in public places, housing, and on public transport. Qualifying disabilities include:

  • Vision impairment (guide dogs)
  • Hearing impairment (hearing dogs)
  • Mobility impairment (mobility assistance dogs)
  • Neurological conditions (seizure alert, diabetes alert dogs)
  • Psychiatric disabilities (PTSD, severe anxiety, autism — where the dog is trained to perform specific tasks)

The key legal test is whether the dog performs specific tasks related to the disability. General emotional support alone is not enough to qualify as an assistance dog.

Recognised Assistance Dog Organisations in NZ

New Zealand has several organisations that train and certify assistance dogs:

  • Blind Low Vision NZ (formerly RNZFB) — Guide dogs for people with vision impairment. The oldest and most established programme in NZ.
  • Hearing Dogs NZ — Trains dogs to alert people with hearing loss to sounds like doorbells, alarms, and phones.
  • Assistance Dogs NZ Trust — Trains dogs for people with physical disabilities, autism, and other conditions.
  • K9 Medical Detection NZ — Trains dogs to detect medical episodes including seizures and diabetic lows.
  • Perfect Partners Assistance Dogs — Owner-trained assistance dogs with professional guidance.
Legal Right: Under the Human Rights Act, it is unlawful to refuse a person with a disability access to any public place, housing, or transport because they are accompanied by an assistance dog. This includes restaurants, shops, hotels, taxis, buses, and domestic flights. The only exception is where the dog poses a genuine risk to health and safety (such as in a sterile surgical environment).

Therapy Dogs in New Zealand

Therapy dogs are dogs that are trained to provide comfort, affection, and emotional support to other people in structured settings such as hospitals, rest homes, schools, disaster recovery centres, and counselling offices. Unlike assistance dogs, they do not have legal public access rights — they can only go where they are invited.

What Makes a Good Therapy Dog?

Not every dog is suited to therapy work. The temperament requirements are demanding:

  • Calm and predictable: Must remain settled in busy, noisy, and unfamiliar environments.
  • Friendly but not overbearing: Should approach people gently without jumping, licking excessively, or seeking attention.
  • Comfortable with handling: Must tolerate being touched all over, including ears, paws, tail, and mouth, by strangers of all ages.
  • Unflappable: Should not react to sudden noises, medical equipment, wheelchairs, walking frames, or unpredictable movements.
  • Reliable obedience: Must sit, stay, come, and walk nicely on lead without repeated commands.
  • Healthy and well-groomed: Must be up to date on vaccinations, worming, and flea treatment, and presented clean at every visit.

Therapy Dog Organisations in NZ

Several organisations coordinate therapy dog visits and assessments in New Zealand:

  • Canine Friends Pet Therapy — One of NZ\'s largest therapy dog organisations, with volunteer teams visiting hospitals, rest homes, prisons, and schools nationwide.
  • Zephyr Animal Assisted Intervention — Provides therapy dog services with a focus on mental health settings.
  • St John Therapy Pets — Operates in partnership with healthcare providers for therapeutic visits.
  • SPCA education programmes — Some SPCA centres run therapy animal visits.
  • Local rest homes and hospitals — Many accept therapy dog visits independently or through community groups.

How to Register Your Dog as a Therapy Dog

The process generally follows these steps:

  1. Assess your dog\'s temperament: Be honest. If your dog is anxious around strangers, reactive to other dogs, or uncomfortable in new environments, therapy work may not be appropriate. It is not a failing — it is simply not the right role for every dog.
  2. Complete basic obedience training: Your dog should reliably respond to sit, stay, come, leave it, and loose-lead walking. A Canine Good Citizen (CGC) certificate or equivalent is a strong foundation.
  3. Apply to a therapy dog organisation: Contact Canine Friends Pet Therapy or another recognised group. They will assess your dog, conduct a temperament test, and may require a health certificate from your vet.
  4. Pass the temperament assessment: This typically involves testing your dog\'s reaction to strangers, handling, noise, other animals, and food left on the floor.
  5. Complete supervised visits: Many organisations require new teams to do several supervised visits before going out independently.
  6. Commit to regular visits: Therapy work relies on consistency. Most organisations ask for a minimum commitment of regular visits over at least six months.
ZiggySitters Tip: If your dog has the right temperament for therapy work but you are not sure where to start, contact Canine Friends Pet Therapy (caninefriends.org.nz). They have regional coordinators across NZ who can guide you through the assessment process and match you with appropriate visiting venues.

Emotional Support Animals: The NZ Reality

Unlike the United States, where emotional support animals have specific legal recognition in housing and air travel, New Zealand has no legal category for ESAs. This means:

  • No public access rights: An ESA dog cannot enter shops, restaurants, or other public places that normally exclude pets, unless the venue chooses to allow it.
  • No housing rights: Landlords are not legally required to make exceptions for ESAs, unlike assistance dogs which must be accommodated under the Human Rights Act.
  • No transport rights: Airlines and public transport operators treat ESAs as regular pets.
  • No certification pathway: There is no government or recognised body that certifies ESAs in NZ. Any website selling "ESA certification" is operating outside NZ law.

What About Doctor\'s Letters?

A letter from your GP, psychologist, or psychiatrist stating that your pet provides emotional support may help in some situations — for example, when requesting permission from a landlord to keep a pet. However, it does not carry legal weight equivalent to an assistance dog certification. A landlord may consider it as part of their decision, but they are not obligated to grant an exception.

If You Need Legal Protection, Consider an Assistance Dog

If you have a genuine psychiatric disability and your dog performs specific tasks to help with that disability, you may qualify for assistance dog status rather than ESA. Examples of psychiatric assistance dog tasks include:

  • Deep pressure therapy during anxiety or panic attacks
  • Interrupting self-harming behaviours
  • Waking a handler from night terrors
  • Creating physical space in crowded environments
  • Retrieving medication or a phone during a crisis
  • Alerting to the onset of a dissociative episode

These are trained tasks, not just the dog\'s presence. If your dog can be trained to perform specific disability-related tasks, the assistance dog pathway provides real legal protection. Contact Assistance Dogs NZ Trust or Perfect Partners Assistance Dogs for guidance.

Training Your Dog for Therapy Work

If you believe your dog has therapy potential, here is a structured path to prepare them:

Foundation Skills (Weeks 1-8)

  • Sit, stay, come, down: Reliable response in low-distraction environments.
  • Loose-lead walking: No pulling, even around distractions.
  • Leave it: Ignore food on the floor, dropped items, and other animals.
  • Place/mat training: Go to a designated spot and settle calmly.
  • Handling tolerance: Practice touching paws, ears, mouth, tail, and body. Reward calm acceptance.

Intermediate Skills (Weeks 9-16)

  • Reliable recall: Come when called in moderate-distraction environments (parks, streets).
  • Greeting manners: Sit calmly when meeting new people instead of jumping.
  • Environmental confidence: Practice in vet clinics, pet-friendly stores, and busy footpaths.
  • Neutral around equipment: Gradually introduce wheelchairs, walking frames, crutches, and medical beeps.
  • Extended settle: Lie quietly for 20-30 minutes without needing to move or interact.

Advanced Preparation (Weeks 17-24)

  • Temperament test practice: Simulate the Canine Friends assessment: sudden noises, unfamiliar people approaching, being crowded, food on the floor, being handled by strangers.
  • Mock visits: Visit friends in rest homes or community centres (with permission) to gauge your dog\'s comfort in these settings.
  • Long-duration calm: Practice 45-60 minute settle periods to prepare for real therapy visits.
  • Multi-person interaction: Have several people interact with your dog simultaneously.
Know When to Stop: If your dog shows signs of stress — lip licking, yawning, whale eye, turning away, panting when not hot, or reluctance to engage — during training or visits, they may not be suited to therapy work. This is not a criticism of your dog. Therapy work is demanding and not every dog enjoys it. Forcing a dog who finds it stressful undermines the purpose.

The Benefits of Animal-Assisted Therapy

Research consistently shows that therapy dog visits provide measurable benefits:

  • In hospitals: Reduced pain perception, lower blood pressure, decreased anxiety, faster recovery times, and improved mood in patients.
  • In aged care: Reduced agitation in dementia patients, increased social interaction, lower rates of depression, and improved physical mobility from petting and walking dogs.
  • In schools: Improved reading confidence (reading to dogs programmes), reduced anxiety, better emotional regulation, and increased school attendance.
  • In mental health settings: Enhanced therapeutic rapport, reduced isolation, greater willingness to engage in treatment, and measurable reductions in stress hormones.
  • In disaster recovery: Critical incident stress debriefing is more effective when therapy dogs are present, and affected communities report feeling more able to talk about their experiences.

Getting Started: What to Do Next

Depending on your situation, here are the concrete next steps:

If You Want to Volunteer with Your Dog as a Therapy Team

  1. Assess your dog honestly against the temperament criteria above.
  2. Complete basic obedience training if your dog is not yet reliable.
  3. Contact Canine Friends Pet Therapy or your local therapy dog group.
  4. Arrange a vet health check and vaccination update.
  5. Attend the temperament assessment.
  6. Complete supervised visits and start volunteering.

If You Need a Dog to Help with Your Disability

  1. Talk to your doctor or specialist about whether an assistance dog is appropriate for your condition.
  2. Contact an assistance dog organisation (Assistance Dogs NZ Trust, Blind Low Vision NZ, Hearing Dogs NZ) to discuss eligibility.
  3. Be prepared for a waiting period — demand far exceeds supply, and wait times can be 12-24 months.
  4. If you want to owner-train, contact Perfect Partners Assistance Dogs for guidance on the process.
  5. Understand your legal rights under the Human Rights Act and be prepared to advocate for yourself if access is challenged.

If You Currently Rely on Your Pet for Emotional Support

  1. Understand that NZ law does not recognise ESAs. You cannot claim public access rights for your pet.
  2. If your pet helps with a genuine disability, explore whether it could be trained for assistance dog tasks — this opens legal protection.
  3. For housing situations, talk to your landlord with a letter from your health professional. Many will consider it, even without a legal obligation.
  4. Avoid paying for online "ESA certification" — it has no legal standing in NZ.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I take my therapy dog into shops and restaurants?

No. Therapy dogs do not have legal public access rights in NZ. They can only enter venues that have specifically invited them for therapy visits. If you want to take your dog into public places that normally exclude pets, the dog must be a certified assistance dog.

Is there a registry for therapy dogs in New Zealand?

There is no central government registry for therapy dogs. Canine Friends Pet Therapy and other therapy organisations maintain their own registers of assessed and active teams. If you want your dog to do therapy work, go through one of these recognised organisations rather than an online registry.

How long does it take to train a therapy dog?

Most dogs need 3-6 months of preparation, assuming they already have basic obedience. The key is temperament, not tricks. A therapy dog needs to be calm, predictable, and genuinely enjoy interacting with strangers. Some dogs are ready sooner; others need more time or may not be suited at all.

Can any breed be a therapy dog?

Yes. While golden retrievers, labradors, and cavalier king charles spaniels are common therapy breeds, the individual dog\'s temperament matters far more than breed. Greyhounds, pit bull types, mixed breeds, and even small dogs can make excellent therapy dogs if they have the right personality and training.

What is the difference between a therapy dog and an assistance dog?

A therapy dog provides comfort to other people in structured settings (hospitals, schools, rest homes). An assistance dog performs specific tasks to help their handler\'s disability and has legal public access rights. They serve entirely different purposes and have different legal standing.

Does NZ recognise emotional support animal letters from overseas?

No. New Zealand does not have an ESA legal framework. An ESA letter from another country has no legal effect in NZ. If you are moving to NZ with an assistance dog, you need certification from an NZ-recognised assistance dog organisation to claim public access rights here.
