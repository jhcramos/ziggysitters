Kitten Sitting in New Zealand: A Practical Care Guide

27 August 2026 · ZiggySitters Team

Kittens are small, curious and surprisingly demanding. If you are leaving New Zealand for a weekend, moving between rentals, or working long shifts, arranging a reliable kitten sitter is not the same as booking a quick cat visit. Young cats need frequent meals, safe play, litter supervision and patient company while they learn the household routine. This guide explains how to prepare your home and choose in-home pet sitting that keeps a kitten settled.

Why kittens need more than a daily check

A healthy adult cat may cope with a single daily visit, but a young kitten can become hungry, frightened or destructive much faster. Depending on age and veterinary advice, kittens often need three or four meals a day. They also need regular litter checks, supervised play and protection from hazards such as cords, string, open windows and other pets. Ask a vet for age-specific guidance, especially if the kitten is newly adopted, underweight or recovering from illness.

Home-based kitten sitting is usually less disruptive than moving a kitten to an unfamiliar cattery. The sitter can preserve feeding times, smells and sleeping places. It also avoids unnecessary travel, which is helpful for kittens still building confidence. A sitter should still be prepared to recommend veterinary care if appetite, toileting, breathing or energy changes.

Choose the right kitten sitter

Look for someone who has genuine experience with kittens, not just adult cats. Ask how they handle biting, hiding, accidents, overnight crying and medication. A good sitter will ask questions about food amounts, litter type, play preferences, vaccination status and the location of the nearest vet. Arrange a meet-and-greet at home before you travel. Let the kitten approach at its own pace; forcing contact is a poor sign.

Confirm the visit schedule in writing. For a very young kitten, several visits or overnight pet sitting may be appropriate. Clarify whether the sitter will stay for a meaningful period or only refill bowls. Discuss photo updates, emergency authority and what happens if the sitter is delayed. Prepare feeding instructions

Leave the exact food, portions and times rather than asking a sitter to estimate. Sudden food changes can cause diarrhoea, and kittens should not be allowed to go without food for extended periods. Use labelled containers for each meal and note whether wet food must be refrigerated after opening. Provide clean bowls, a measuring scoop and spare food for delays. Keep fresh water available in more than one stable bowl.

Do not introduce treats, raw foods or supplements while you are away unless your vet has advised them. If medication is required, demonstrate it during the handover and leave written instructions. The sitter should record doses and contact you if a dose is refused; they should never double a dose without veterinary direction.

Litter, sleep and play

Show the sitter every litter tray and explain the usual appearance and frequency of urine and stools. Kittens can avoid a dirty or inaccessible tray, so daily scooping is essential. Keep trays away from food and noisy appliances. Leave the same litter the kitten already uses, plus cleaning supplies and spare litter. A sudden change can create avoidable accidents.

Provide a quiet safe room with a bed, hiding box, scratching surface and toys. Wand toys, balls and puzzle feeders can provide enrichment, but string, ribbon and feather toys should be stored away after supervised play. Never leave a kitten alone with loose thread or elastic. Short, frequent play sessions are usually better than one exhausting session. The sitter should allow naps and avoid waking a sleeping kitten unnecessarily.

Kitten-proof the New Zealand home

Before leaving, secure flyscreens, balconies, windows and doors. Remove lilies and other toxic plants, medicines, cleaning products, small objects and exposed electrical cables. Close washing machines, dryers, cupboards and toilets. Check recliner chairs and sofa mechanisms before use. If the kitten can access a garage, shed or garden, explain boundaries clearly; antifreeze, rodent bait, fertilisers and tools can be dangerous.

Keep the carrier accessible, not buried in storage. Write down the address and phone number of your chosen vet, an after-hours clinic, your microchip details and your emergency contact. If you live in an earthquake-prone area, keep the carrier, food and a paper instruction sheet together. For rural homes, add gate codes and warnings about ponds, farm machinery and dogs.

Vaccinations, outdoor access and other pets

Ask your veterinarian when outdoor access is appropriate. An unvaccinated or partially vaccinated kitten should generally remain indoors according to veterinary advice. Do not ask a sitter to take a kitten outside because it seems bored. Windows can provide safe stimulation when securely screened. If the kitten lives with a dog or older cat, explain feeding separation and never assume they can be left together unsupervised.

Introduce animals slowly: separate spaces, scent exchange and brief supervised contact are safer than immediate free access. Tell the sitter where each pet can retreat. Warning signs include persistent hiding, hissing, chasing, refusal to eat and toileting changes. The sitter should separate animals and contact you rather than trying to force friendship.

Emergency plan and handover checklist

Leave written permission for the sitter to contact your vet and state the spending limit you authorise for urgent care. Include your travel dates, backup contact, feeding chart, medication log, litter routine, alarm instructions and rubbish collection details. Ask for a message after each visit and immediate contact for repeated vomiting, breathing difficulty, collapse, suspected poisoning, injury or refusal to eat. These are not situations to wait out.

Do a trial visit before departure. Check that the sitter can locate supplies, operate the carrier and follow the feeding plan. At the final handover, update them about any change in appetite or behaviour. A calm, consistent routine is the best gift you can leave a kitten.
