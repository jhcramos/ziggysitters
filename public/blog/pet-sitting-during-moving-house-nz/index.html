Pet Sitting During a House Move in New Zealand: A Stress-Free Plan

Published on 30 August 2026 | By ZiggySitters

Moving house is exciting for people and confusing for pets. Boxes appear, furniture disappears and unfamiliar people walk through the front door. Dogs can become anxious or escape through an open gate, while cats may hide for hours or stop eating. A reliable pet sitter gives your animal a calm, secure base while you manage the move. This guide explains how to plan pet sitting in New Zealand before, during and after moving day.

Why a house move is difficult for pets

Pets understand home through scent, routine and familiar territory. Packing removes those signals one room at a time. Noise from movers, doors left open and changes to feeding or walking times add further stress. Even a confident Labrador can bolt when a removal truck arrives. Cats are particularly vulnerable because they often associate territory with safety and may try to return to the old address.

Keeping your pet away from the busiest part of the move is not overprotective. It prevents injuries from lifting equipment, broken glass, open vehicle doors and accidental exposure to cleaning products. It also lets you focus on logistics instead of checking whether a frightened animal is underfoot.

Choose the right care arrangement

Start by deciding how long your pet needs care. For a small local move, a sitter can provide a day visit or look after your pet overnight. For an interstate relocation, a longer stay may be better. Options include in-home pet sitting at the old property, house sitting at the new property once it is safe, or boarding with a trusted sitter.

Home-based care is often the least disruptive option for dogs that rely on their normal yard and walks. Cats usually benefit from remaining in one secure location until the move is complete. If the old house will be empty and echoing, arrange a sitter’s home or a quiet cattery-style alternative rather than leaving the cat alone among boxes.

On ZiggySitters, describe the move clearly in your booking request. Mention dates, travel distance, stairs, fencing, other animals and whether your pet needs medication. A sitter who understands the situation can prepare properly.

Book early and arrange a meet and greet

Moving dates change, but that is not a reason to wait. Experienced pet sitters are in demand around summer holidays, Christmas and the end of rental tenancies. Book a provisional date if possible and confirm it when your settlement or truck booking is final.

Use the meet and greet to show the sitter your pet’s routine, favourite hiding places, lead, carrier and medication. Explain how your pet behaves when stressed. Does your dog bark at strangers? Does your cat slip through doors? Specific information is more useful than saying an animal is “usually good”. Leave written instructions and an emergency contact who can make decisions if you are unreachable.

Prepare a moving-day safety zone

If your pet stays at home on packing day, create one closed room before the first box is loaded. Put in water, food, bedding, litter, toys and a sign asking movers not to enter. Check the window, doors and flyscreens. A sitter can remain with your pet in this room, but the safest choice for many families is to have the animal elsewhere before movers arrive.

Never rely on a loose “please watch the door” arrangement. Removalists are concentrating on heavy furniture, not on a nervous terrier. Ensure your pet wears a collar with current contact details, even if microchipped. Microchips are valuable, but they do not prevent a finder from needing a phone number quickly.

Give your sitter a complete care pack

  • Several days of the usual food, measured portions and treats
  • Medication, written doses and your regular vet’s details
  • Lead, harness, carrier, bedding and familiar toys
  • Microchip and council registration details
  • Emergency contact and permission for veterinary treatment
  • New address, move timetable and transport instructions
  • Advice about fears, triggers, escape routes and toilet habits

Do not introduce a new diet or calming product immediately before moving day unless your veterinarian recommends it. Familiar food and predictable handling reduce variables when you are already dealing with a major change.

Transport pets safely to the new home

Move your pet after the heavy work is finished. Dogs should travel restrained with a suitable harness, crate or barrier; cats should travel in a secure carrier, never loose in the vehicle. For a long drive between North and South Island, plan toilet breaks, water and temperature control. Never leave an animal in a parked car, even on a mild day.

If a pet sitter transports your animal, confirm who supplies the vehicle, carrier and fuel, and whether the sitter is comfortable with the journey. Keep vaccination, medication and vet information accessible rather than packed in the moving truck.

Set up one room first

At the new property, prepare a quiet room before your pet arrives. Add familiar bedding, water, food and a litter tray. Keep doors and windows closed while the animal adjusts. Let a dog explore gradually and keep it on lead in an unfamiliar yard until fencing and gates have been checked. For cats, start with one room for at least the first day or two, then expand access at their pace.

Check hazards specific to New Zealand homes: unsecured gates, decks, ponds, water tanks, fireplaces, rat bait, toxic garden plants and gaps under fences. Find the nearest vet and update your address with your council and microchip registry. For rural homes, inspect stock yards, dams and driveways before allowing free outdoor access.

Help your pet settle

Return to the old routine quickly: meals at familiar times, normal walks and the same words for toilet or bedtime. Expect temporary behaviours such as hiding, vocalising, reduced appetite or indoor accidents. Give your pet quiet attention, but do not force interaction. A sitter can provide continuity during the first night if work or travel prevents you being present.

Contact your veterinarian if your pet will not eat or drink, has repeated vomiting or diarrhoea, struggles to breathe, appears injured, or shows severe ongoing distress. Behaviour changes after a move are common, but serious symptoms should not be dismissed as stress.

Final moving checklist

  • Pet sitter booked with backup contact confirmed
  • Pet care pack labelled and kept out of the moving truck
  • Collar, identification and microchip details checked
  • Safe room arranged at both properties
  • Carrier or vehicle restraint ready
  • New property gates and hazards inspected
  • Vet, council and microchip address updated
  • First week’s routine written down

A house move does not need to become a traumatic event for your pet. With a sitter, a secure transport plan and a gradual introduction to the new home, you can protect their wellbeing while getting the practical work done.
