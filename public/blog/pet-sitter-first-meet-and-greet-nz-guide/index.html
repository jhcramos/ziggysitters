Pet Sitter Meet and Greet in NZ: Questions to Ask

Published 23 August 2026 · 8 min read · ZiggySitters NZ

The first meeting with a pet sitter is not just a friendly introduction. It is your chance to check compatibility, discuss safety and make sure the sitter understands your dog, cat or small pet before you travel. These questions help New Zealand pet owners make a confident choice.

Why a meet-and-greet matters

Pets respond to people differently. A dog may be relaxed on a walk but protective at home; a cat may hide from every visitor and need a quiet, patient approach. Meeting in the pet’s normal environment reveals how the sitter listens, moves around the home and responds to your animal’s signals. It also gives you a chance to explain routines that cannot be captured in a short profile.

For regular pet sitting, the first meeting is the foundation of a relationship. For one-off holiday care, it reduces uncertainty for everyone. Keep the meeting calm and allow your pet to approach in their own time.

Questions about experience and fit

Ask what kinds of animals the sitter has cared for, how long they have been pet sitting and whether they have handled medication, puppies, senior pets or anxious animals. Experience is useful, but a sitter who is honest about their limits is safer than someone who says yes to everything. Ask whether they are comfortable with your pet’s size, breed, mobility needs and behaviour.

  • Have you cared for a pet with this routine or medication before?
  • How would you respond if my pet hides, refuses food or becomes distressed?
  • Are you comfortable with multiple pets, birds, rabbits or reptiles?
  • What would make you contact me, the vet or an emergency contact?

Check the daily care plan

Describe a typical day and ask the sitter to explain it back. Discuss meal times, toilet breaks, walks, play, grooming and sleep. Be specific about off-lead exercise, dog parks, beaches and introductions to other animals. In New Zealand, local conditions matter: a walk that is safe in a suburban reserve may be unsuitable during heavy rain, high winds, slips or extreme heat.

Ask how the sitter will keep your pet secure when opening gates or the front door. If your dog pulls, your cat bolts or your pet needs a carrier, agree on the exact equipment and handling method. A competent sitter should be willing to follow your safety rules even if they would do things differently with their own animal.

Discuss health and emergency decisions

Show the sitter your pet’s medication, food and first-aid supplies. Confirm dose, timing and storage. Give written vet details, microchip information, insurance details if relevant and permission to obtain treatment. Agree on who can authorise care if you cannot answer and what spending limit applies.

Ask what the sitter would do for a missing pet, injury, suspected poisoning or sudden illness. The right answer is calm containment, prompt professional advice and clear communication—not waiting hours for a message. Do not expect a sitter to diagnose conditions or provide human medication.

Be honest about behaviour: Mention fear, guarding, escape attempts, bite history, reactivity and triggers. Hiding a problem puts the animal and sitter at risk and makes a booking a poor fit.

Agree on communication

Decide how often you want updates and what format works. Some owners prefer a daily photo; others want a short message only when something changes. Give the sitter a primary number, an alternate contact and your travel details. Ask what happens if the sitter loses phone service, becomes ill or cannot reach the property.

A simple written care sheet should include the address, access instructions, feeding schedule, routines, medication, vet, emergency contact, house rules and evacuation plan. Leave both a printed copy and a digital version. Confirm the sitter will protect your privacy and not share photos or details publicly without permission.

Clarify practical booking details

Confirm dates, arrival windows, visit frequency, overnight arrangements, cancellation terms and what happens if your return is delayed. Ask whether the sitter has a backup plan for illness or transport problems. For house sitting, clarify which rooms and appliances are included; for visits, confirm how long each visit lasts and what is included in the service.

Discuss keys, lockboxes, alarms, parking, apartment access and any security cameras. Cameras should be disclosed and used lawfully; nobody should be surprised by monitoring in private spaces. Agree on how keys are returned and whether a trusted local person can help with access.

Watch the interaction, not just the answers

Notice whether your pet can settle around the sitter and whether the sitter respects distance and body language. A cat does not need to come out immediately, and a nervous dog does not need to be forced into a greeting. Look for calm handling, thoughtful questions and accurate notes. Be cautious if someone rushes, ignores your instructions, minimises a concern or promises to manage a need they have not understood.

Good test: Ask the sitter to demonstrate how they would attach the harness, prepare a meal or secure the front door. Practical clarity is more valuable than a polished sales pitch.

Make the decision

After the meeting, ask yourself three questions: Did the sitter understand my pet’s needs? Would my pet be safe with them? Can we communicate easily if plans change? Price matters, but the cheapest option is expensive if it leads to an escape, missed medication or an emergency handled badly.

If the fit is right, confirm everything in writing and book early for summer, Christmas, Easter and school holidays, when demand for pet sitting in New Zealand is higher. A short trial visit can be sensible before a long trip, especially for puppies, senior pets or animals with anxiety.
