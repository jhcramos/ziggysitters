Senior Cat Pet Sitting in New Zealand: A Practical Care Guide

Published on 30 August 2026 | By ZiggySitters

Older cats often prefer quiet routines, familiar rooms and a small circle of trusted people. That can make leaving them during a holiday feel worrying, especially if they take medication or have arthritis, kidney disease or reduced vision. The right senior cat pet sitter can keep care consistent at home and spot changes early. This New Zealand guide covers what to look for, what to prepare and when a sitter should call your vet.

Why senior cats need tailored pet sitting

A healthy young cat may cope with a missed meal or a changed schedule. A senior cat may not. Older cats can become dehydrated more quickly, struggle to reach a high water bowl or hide signs of illness. A sitter should therefore do more than top up food: they should observe appetite, water intake, litter habits, movement and behaviour.

Staying at home is often less stressful than travelling to a new environment. The cat keeps familiar smells, hiding places and temperature. This is especially helpful for cats with anxiety, sensory decline or chronic conditions. A sitter can provide daily visits or stay overnight, depending on the cat’s needs and your veterinarian’s advice.

Choose experience over a generic drop-in

When interviewing a sitter, ask whether they have cared for older cats and administered medication. Ask how they would respond if the cat refuses food, vomits, misses a dose or cannot get into the litter tray. You want calm observation and a clear escalation plan, not guesswork.

Explain your cat’s normal behaviour at the meet and greet. Some senior cats sleep most of the day; others become vocal at night. A sitter who knows the baseline can identify a meaningful change. Decide how often visits are needed

For a robust older cat with no medication, one or two visits daily may be suitable if your veterinarian agrees. Cats with diabetes, multiple medications, poor appetite or recent illness may need more frequent visits or overnight care. Do not choose the cheapest schedule automatically: a gap that is acceptable for one cat could be unsafe for another.

Ask the sitter to arrive at roughly the normal meal times. Consistency helps cats feel secure and makes it easier to notice whether food has actually been eaten. If the cat is allowed outdoors, consider keeping it inside while you are away. Older cats may be slower to escape hazards, and a sitter may not be able to locate them quickly in an unfamiliar situation.

Prepare a clear senior-cat care plan

  • Food type, measured quantity and feeding times
  • Water bowl locations and whether water needs refreshing
  • Medication name, dose, timing and administration method
  • Regular vet, after-hours clinic and your emergency contact
  • Normal appetite, toileting, sleeping and grooming habits
  • Mobility limits, pain signs and safe areas to access
  • Carrier location, transport permission and payment arrangement

Write instructions in plain language and demonstrate medication rather than leaving only a label. Include what to do if a tablet is dropped or spat out. The sitter should not change a dose without veterinary direction. Keep medicines together, clearly labelled and away from children and other animals.

Make the home easier for an older cat

Reduce the distance between essential resources. Put food and water on the same level as the cat’s favourite resting area. Use a low-sided litter tray if stepping over a high edge is difficult, but do not change litter type immediately before your trip. Non-slip rugs can help on polished floors, and a warm bed away from draughts is useful during a cold Canterbury or Otago winter.

Block unsafe access to balconies, open windows and steep stairs. Check that doors cannot close a cat into a cupboard or garage. Remove lilies, medicines, cleaning products and accessible cords. In warmer North Island weather, provide shade and ventilation; in winter, ensure heaters and fireplaces are secure and supervised.

Support medication and chronic conditions

Common senior-cat needs include thyroid medication, renal diets, insulin, joint support and regular monitoring. The sitter must know whether a treatment is essential at a precise time and what constitutes an emergency. Ask your vet for written instructions if the condition is complex. Leave enough supplies for the full booking plus a small buffer, but avoid making medication changes solely for convenience.

For a diabetic cat, do not assume a sitter can safely manage injections without training. Arrange a demonstration and a backup plan with your veterinary clinic. If your cat has chronic kidney disease, leave the usual food and note drinking and urination patterns. The sitter is not expected to diagnose, but they should report a substantial deviation promptly.

Use daily observation, not just photos

Agree on a short daily update. A useful report says what the cat ate, whether the litter tray was used, whether medication was given and how the cat moved or interacted. A photo is reassuring but cannot replace observation. Ask the sitter to check the cat’s eyes, breathing, coat and general responsiveness without forcing handling.

Tell the sitter where the cat normally hides. Hiding for a few hours may be normal, but a cat that cannot be found, is unusually weak, repeatedly vomits, has difficulty breathing or has not eaten should trigger a call to you and, where appropriate, a veterinarian. Leave permission and financial arrangements in writing so care is not delayed while someone searches for approval.

Emergency preparation for New Zealand homes

New Zealand households should plan for earthquakes, storms, flooding and power cuts. Keep a small pet emergency kit with food, water, medication, a carrier, a torch, towels and copies of veterinary records. Show the sitter how to secure the cat during an evacuation. A cat may hide after an earthquake, so the carrier should be accessible and the sitter should know the safest rooms.

For rural properties, include directions that do not rely entirely on mobile coverage. For apartments, explain building access, lifts and the body corporate emergency procedure. Leave a neighbour’s number and a backup key holder in case roads are blocked or the sitter cannot reach you.

Before you leave: the meet-and-greet test

Invite the sitter to complete a trial visit while you are still home. Watch whether they approach slowly, respect the cat’s boundaries and follow instructions. Let them practise opening the carrier and giving medication if required. This is the moment to discover that your cat refuses a particular pill technique or that the sitter cannot manage the stairs—not on the morning of your flight.

Keep the routine unchanged during the trial. Your cat should have the opportunity to approach voluntarily. A good sitter does not need to force affection; reliable care is more important than a dramatic bond on the first meeting.

When home sitting is not enough

Home pet sitting may not suit a cat needing intensive clinical monitoring, continuous nursing or treatment that a sitter is not trained to provide. In those cases, discuss veterinary boarding or another professional option with your vet. The goal is safe care, not proving that every holiday arrangement must happen at home.

Senior cat sitting checklist

  • Book a sitter with senior-cat and medication experience
  • Complete a trial visit and meet and greet
  • Write down normal behaviour and medical instructions
  • Place food, water, litter and bedding within easy reach
  • Secure windows, balconies, heaters and hazardous products
  • Leave vet contacts, emergency authorisation and backup key
  • Arrange daily updates with appetite and litter observations
  • Prepare an earthquake and power-cut emergency kit

With thoughtful preparation, your older cat can remain comfortable in familiar surroundings while you travel.
