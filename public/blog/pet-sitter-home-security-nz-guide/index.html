Preparing Your Home for a Pet Sitter in New Zealand: Safety Guide

Published 23 August 2026 · 8 min read · ZiggySitters NZ

A good pet sitter needs more than feeding instructions. A home that is easy to access, secure and set up for your animal helps prevent escapes, accidents and last-minute confusion while you are away. Use this New Zealand pet sitting checklist before handing over the keys.

Start with a practical home walk-through

Walk through the property at your pet’s height and look for anything a visitor might miss. Check gates, latches, flyscreens, balcony doors and loose fence panels. Dogs can push through surprisingly small gaps, while cats can find open windows and roof access routes. Tell your sitter which doors must stay closed and whether tradespeople, cleaners or gardeners are expected.

Show the sitter the normal route from the entrance to the pet’s living area. Remove clutter from hallways, secure electrical cords and store cleaning products, fertiliser and bait out of reach. If your pet is allowed on furniture or in certain rooms, write that down rather than relying on assumptions.

Make access secure and simple

Choose one reliable access method: a lockbox, smart lock, key held by a trusted local contact, or a clear handover. Test it before departure. A spare key hidden under a pot is not a secure plan. Never leave alarm codes in a public note; share them privately and explain how to disarm and re-arm the system.

Give the sitter a current emergency contact who is actually in New Zealand if possible. Include the property address, parking instructions, rubbish collection details and any building or body corporate rules. For apartments, explain lifts, swipe cards, visitor parking and the procedure if the card stops working.

Useful handover: Leave a one-page “arrive and leave” list: lock the back door, check the cat flap, confirm water, set the alarm and send a short arrival message.

Prepare food, water and medication

Pre-portion food where practical and label containers with the pet’s name and meal time. Include treats, supplements and instructions for days when the animal refuses food. Show the sitter where spare supplies are stored and leave enough for the booking plus a few extra days in case flights or weather disrupt your return.

Write medication instructions in plain language: dose, timing, method and what to do if a dose is missed or vomited. Demonstrate tablets, ear drops or injections in advance. Record the vet’s name, phone number, after-hours clinic and permission to seek treatment. Never ask a sitter to give human medicine.

Reduce common household hazards

Put rubbish bins behind a closed door or secure lid. Remove toxic plants, chocolate, grapes, onions, xylitol products and accessible medicines. Close the washing machine and dryer, check toilet lids and keep strings, fishing line and small toys away from animals that chew. If your pet has a history of counter-surfing, clear benches before leaving.

New Zealand homes have different seasonal risks. In winter, explain heaters, fireplaces and electric blankets. In summer, identify the coolest room and never leave a pet in a parked car. During wet weather, provide a towel and safe indoor enrichment rather than asking a sitter to take a dog onto a flooded track or slippery slope.

Leave clear pet behaviour and routine notes

Tell the sitter what normal looks like: where the dog sleeps, how the cat asks for food, how often the rabbit’s enclosure is checked, and what noises cause fear. Note triggers, body-language warnings and safe ways to handle the animal. Honest detail is helpful; “friendly” does not mean every pet enjoys strangers, children or other dogs.

  • Daily feeding, toileting, exercise and enrichment schedule
  • Favourite hiding places and whether the pet should be coaxed or left alone
  • Lead, harness, carrier and muzzle locations, if relevant
  • Rules for visitors, off-lead areas and contact with other animals
  • Photo and update preferences, including what counts as urgent

Plan for power, weather and emergencies

Leave a torch, power bank, spare batteries and paper contact details where they can be found. Identify the main water shut-off, fuse box and gas isolation point, but do not ask a sitter to perform dangerous repairs. Explain what to do if the smoke alarm sounds, the power fails or the property becomes unsafe: evacuate people first, secure the pet if possible, and contact emergency services or the nominated local contact.

For rural properties, include gate codes, stock hazards, ponds, farm machinery and areas that are out of bounds. For coastal homes, mention tsunami evacuation routes. In earthquake-prone areas, keep carriers and leads accessible rather than buried in a cupboard. A sitter should know where to meet if the home cannot be re-entered.

Do a proper handover before you leave

Invite the sitter over while you are still home. Demonstrate feeding, medication, alarm use and the safest way to enter and leave. Let the pet meet them without pressure. Ask the sitter to repeat the important points back to you; this catches ambiguity before it becomes an emergency.

Leave printed instructions as well as a digital copy. Include your itinerary, return time, alternate contact, vet authorisation and a recent photo of each pet. Update the sheet after every booking if a routine, medication or access detail changes.

Red flags that need a call: breathing trouble, collapse, uncontrolled bleeding, suspected poisoning, repeated vomiting, inability to urinate, heat stress or severe injury. Tell the sitter to seek veterinary advice promptly rather than waiting for a reply from an overseas phone.

A calmer booking for everyone

Preparing the home is part of responsible pet care. When access, supplies, routines and emergency decisions are clear, your sitter can focus on the animal instead of solving household puzzles. It also makes in-home pet sitting more consistent for pets who are happiest in familiar surroundings.
