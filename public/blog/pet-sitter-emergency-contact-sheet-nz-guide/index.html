Pet Sitter Emergency Contact Sheet NZ: What to Leave

27 August 2026 · ZiggySitters Team

Most pet sitting arrangements run smoothly. The value of an emergency contact sheet is that it removes guesswork when something unexpected happens: a pet refuses food, a gate blows open, a storm cuts power or a sitter cannot reach you. A good sheet is short enough to use under pressure, accurate enough for a New Zealand veterinary clinic and stored where the sitter can find it immediately.

Put the essentials at the top

Start with a one-page summary. Write each pet's name, species, age, sex, colour, microchip number and any important behaviour such as fear of strangers or escape attempts. Add your full name, travel dates, accommodation or overseas phone number and the name of a backup decision-maker. Include the home's full address and a clear instruction about where the carrier, lead and first-aid supplies are kept.

Do not rely only on a phone contact. Phones lose charge, numbers change and a sitter may need details while standing at the property. Print the sheet and leave a digital copy in the agreed message thread. Tell the sitter which version is current.

Veterinary and emergency contacts

List your regular veterinary clinic, phone number, address and opening hours. Add the nearest after-hours clinic and the National Poisons Centre number in New Zealand, 0800 POISON (0800 764 766), for urgent poisoning advice. In a life-threatening emergency, the sitter should contact a vet or emergency service immediately rather than waiting for your reply. The sheet should say whether you authorise transport and treatment when you cannot be reached.

Include your preferred payment method and any insurance policy details, but never put full banking passwords on a public-looking document. Confirm with the clinic how your consent and payment instructions will be handled. Give the sitter permission in writing to share relevant medical history with the veterinarian.

Medication and medical history

For every medicine, record the name, strength, dose, route, exact time and whether it is given with food. Note allergies, previous reactions and the date of the last dose. Keep medicines in original labelled packaging and separate from human medicines. A simple tick-box log prevents accidental double dosing, particularly when more than one person may visit.

Summarise important conditions in plain language: diabetes, seizures, kidney disease, mobility limits or recent surgery. Explain the pet's normal appetite, toilet habits, breathing and energy so a sitter can identify a meaningful change. Leave enough medication for the whole booking plus a small buffer, and show the sitter the technique before you leave. Never ask an untrained sitter to perform a procedure they are not comfortable doing.

What counts as an emergency?

Write specific escalation instructions. Immediate veterinary advice is appropriate for collapse, difficulty breathing, uncontrolled bleeding, suspected toxin ingestion, a serious injury, repeated vomiting, a swollen abdomen, inability to urinate or a pet that is unusually unresponsive. For cats, not passing urine can be an emergency. For dogs, heat stress can worsen quickly during warm weather. The sitter should not diagnose from internet searches.

For less urgent issues, state when you want a call: refusing a meal, mild diarrhoea, a lost toy, a broken latch or a small accident. Agree on communication expectations before departure, such as one daily update and immediate calls for red flags. Include a backup contact who can make decisions if you are unreachable.

Home, weather and evacuation details

Pet emergencies can begin with the property. Record alarm instructions, spare key location, water shut-off, fuse box, gas isolation point and the number of a trusted neighbour. Explain what to do if there is a power failure, fire alarm, flood or earthquake: protect people first, secure pets only if safe, leave the property and call emergency services. Do not ask a sitter to enter an unsafe building.

For coastal homes, note the local tsunami evacuation route. For rural or lifestyle-block properties, identify gates, stock, ponds, farm equipment, sheds and pest-control products. For homes with fireplaces, swimming pools or balconies, make the hazards explicit. Leave torches, a power bank and paper contacts where they can be reached without searching.

Make the sheet easy to use

  • Use large headings and one section per topic.
  • Write dates as 27 August 2026 rather than 27/08/26.
  • Use tick boxes for feeding, medication and visits.
  • Include photos or simple descriptions for pets who look alike.
  • Mark doors, carriers and medication with labels.
  • Review every number and instruction at the meet-and-greet.

Keep the sheet beside the main pet supplies, and tell the sitter exactly where it is. Avoid hiding emergency instructions in a long message history. If your itinerary changes, send an updated page rather than scattered corrections.

Test the plan before travelling

Ask the sitter to talk through three scenarios: the pet vomits, the power fails and you cannot be reached. If they do not know whom to call or where the carrier is, improve the sheet. A short trial visit also reveals practical problems such as a stiff gate, missing medication syringe or a phone number that does not connect.

Good preparation does not mean expecting disaster. It means giving a capable sitter the authority and information to act quickly while preserving your pet's routine. Once the sheet is ready, save it as part of your regular holiday checklist and update it whenever your vet, address or emergency contact changes.
